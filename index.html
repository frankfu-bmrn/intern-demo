What twelve weeks actually taught me.

Not a recap of the walkthrough. Where I started, what changed my thinking, and what I would do with more time.

01 — Where I startedI assumed the data would be the hard part.

The brief was to make shipment performance visible. I expected the difficulty to sit in the modeling — joining sources, settling definitions, getting the logic right.

That work was real. It was not where the time went, and it was not what mattered most.

02 — What changed my thinkingOne shipment reframed the whole project.

A batch moved before full release and then sat at its destination against an open deviation. Nothing in the records was wrong. The movement data was right. The quality data was right.

They were simply held apart. Proving why that shipment was late meant asking several people across two functions and waiting on replies.

That was the moment the project stopped being about a dashboard. Teams act on these every time — they just start later, with less room left to stop the next one.

03 — What it costMost of the time went to retrieval, not building.

Finding which system held the truth. Discovering two teams meant different things by "late." Working out who to ask, then waiting. Building came last and took the least.

I do not read that as a story about being new. Anyone reconstructing the same answer pays the same cost, every time it is needed — and off-plan events happen often enough that this is a recurring bill, not a one-off.

04 — What I would do with more timeThree steps, in order.

  • Agree the definitions. One meaning of late, one owner per metric. Contributions do not stack until the words match.
  • Link movement to quality context at batch level, keeping unmatched cases visible rather than quietly dropped.
  • Record the decision against the number — cause, owner, action, outcome — so the next person does not rebuild it from scratch.

None of this needs a new platform. It extends what already exists.

05 — Where this goes nextOne thread is a proof, not a program.

Shipment on-time is one tile on a board full of them. The connection underneath it — movement, quality context, decision — is the same shape for most of the others. What I built is a worked example of it, not the end of it.

Once that record exists, the questions change from backward to forward:

  • Which batches an open deviation puts at risk, before the next shipment is planned.
  • Which shipments in flight are trending toward a miss, while there is still room to move.
  • Which to prioritize, weighted by how critical each external partner is to supply.
  • What drives most delay across the whole network, rather than one lane at a time.

All of these are answerable today. They cost days of asking, which is why they get asked after the fact instead of before it. Closing that gap is what turns reporting into prevention.

It is also the groundwork for a serious AI use case here. Not a model fixing a metric — a connected record of what moved, what happened to it, and what was decided. That record is the prerequisite, and it does not exist yet. I would build it next.

06 — What I am taking with meNot the technical skill.

What I want to carry forward is not the modeling or the tooling. It is the environment, and the people who made the work possible.

Biotech is one of the few industries where the constraints are genuinely real and the possibilities are still wide open at the same time — heavily regulated, and sitting on technology that can change how the work actually gets done. That combination is what I want to keep working in.

And the way this place operates stayed with me: be real, have fun, and keep doing both in a market that does not slow down for anyone. That is what I would be glad to keep contributing to.
